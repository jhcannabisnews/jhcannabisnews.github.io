EPISODE 38 · FRI NOV 20, 2026

The fight to save hemp just got harder: 2 senators vow to block it

Sens. Ted Budd (R-NC) and Pete Ricketts (R-NE) say they won't support any year-end funding package that extends, delays or builds rules for hemp THC products. Here is what they said, how senators voted in August, and what two senators can and can't do.

Why the funding bill matters: Dec 11 is when the federal ban on most hemp THC products starts and when government funding runs out. The U.S. Hemp Roundtable wants the fix attached to that bill. With 21 days left, time is the senators' real power.

What the senators actually said

They did not say "block"; they said they will not support it. Sen. Ted Budd (R-NC) posted that he "will not support any year-end package that extends, delays, or builds a regulatory framework around" these products, adding that Congress closed the hemp loophole more than a year ago and that constituents have told him loved ones' addictions began with them. That evening Sen. Pete Ricketts (R-NE) wrote: "I'm with Budd on this." Both say they will not back extending, delaying or regulating.

The rescue bill they are reacting to has one sponsor from each party, Sen. Tim Sheehy (R-MT) and Sen. Amy Klobuchar (D-MN). Budd says it would legalize these products at higher potency. Its sponsors call it guardrails: it raises the hemp line to 1 percent total THC, scraps the 0.4 mg per-container cap with no new milligram number, bars more than trace amounts of delta-8 and similar compounds, sets 21 and up, and has the FDA write testing and packaging rules. See our Hemp Guardrails Act explainer.

The last time Budd tried: 61-32

In August, while the Senate passed the stopgap that moved the ban to Dec 11, Budd filed an amendment to strike the hemp delay. At 2:36 a.m. on Aug 8, the Senate voted 61 to 32 to table it, so the delay stayed. Ricketts voted with Budd. Sheehy, Klobuchar and Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY) voted to keep the delay. Texas's senators split: Sen. John Cornyn (R) voted with Budd and Sen. Ted Cruz (R) voted to keep the delay. About an hour later the whole stopgap passed 90 to 6, with Budd and Ricketts both voting yes; the House passed it 370 to 48. Now both say they won't support a bill that delays, and writing a full set of rules is a bigger ask than a one-month delay.

The rescue route: the funding bill

Rep. James Comer (R-KY) told the Kentucky Hemp Association on a call, WKMS/WEKU reported, that the fix must "get this on a bill that is going to pass," which Marijuana Moment says means the stopgap needed by Dec 11. Comer said a standalone bill would take too long and that "I think we've got to have lots of alternatives." Kentucky's hemp market is worth about $300 million, per the station. As of Thursday, nobody had announced that hemp language is in the next funding bill, and we found no committee hearing or vote on the Sheehy-Klobuchar bill. It is a hope, not a plan.

Can two senators stop it?

Not alone. Most Senate business moves by unanimous consent, so any one senator can object. The Congressional Research Service describes a "hold" as an informal notice, not a veto. If someone objects, the majority leader can still bring a bill up, but ending debate takes 60 of 100 votes and each round takes days of floor time. So Budd and Ricketts can object, force extra votes and burn the clock, but they cannot stop a bill that 60 senators want to move. Our read: House leaders decide what goes in the bill, and two firm no votes make it easier to leave hemp out. If the fix is included, Budd can try again to strike it.

If nothing passes on Dec 11, hemp is measured by total THC including THCA, most THCA flower stops being hemp, gummies and drinks over 0.4 mg per container are out, and Spectrum News reported no grace period for leftover stock.

Both sides

Ban backers say these products drive addiction and that kids and the workforce pay the price; that is Budd's case. Regulation backers say a ban moves the market off the shelf rather than ending it, and that age limits, testing and labels do more to protect kids. Texans: your two senators voted differently in August, so tell both where you stand using senate.gov, and use our Contact Lawmakers page for the Legislature, which returns January 12.
