What "Schedule I" actually means

You'll hear it in almost every cannabis story. Here's what the label means, and what moving marijuana to Schedule III would and wouldn't change.

The five schedules

The federal Controlled Substances Act of 1970 sorts drugs into five schedules, I through V, based on abuse potential and medical use.

  • Schedule I: high potential for abuse, no currently accepted medical use, and no accepted safety for use under medical supervision. Examples: heroin, LSD, MDMA, and marijuana.
  • Schedule II: high abuse potential but accepted medical use, such as fentanyl and oxycodone.
  • Schedule III: moderate abuse potential, such as ketamine, anabolic steroids and codeine combination products.
  • Schedules IV and V: progressively lower risk, such as Xanax and low-dose cough medicines.

Marijuana's spot

Marijuana has been Schedule I since the law passed in 1970, and outside state-licensed medical programs it still is. That's the root of the state–federal conflict: a state can legalize recreational use, but federally that marijuana is in the same category as heroin.

The rescheduling push

  • 2023: federal health officials recommended moving marijuana to Schedule III.
  • 2024: the Justice Department proposed the change; the hearing process then stalled.
  • Dec 2025: President Trump signed an executive order to speed up rescheduling to Schedule III.
  • April 2026: the acting Attorney General moved state-licensed medical marijuana and FDA-approved cannabis drugs to Schedule III. Everything else, including recreational marijuana, stayed Schedule I. A court challenge is pending.
  • Sept 29, 2026: the DEA's chief administrative law judge paused the hearing on rescheduling the rest of marijuana, after opponents cited a new federal watchdog (GAO) report. The government's response was due October 13.

What full Schedule III would and wouldn't do

Would: make research much easier, and end the federal tax rule (section 280E) that blocks state-legal cannabis businesses from deducting normal expenses, a huge cost for the industry.

Wouldn't: legalize recreational marijuana federally, make dispensaries federally legal, or change your state's laws.

States have their own schedules

Each state keeps its own list. That's how Texas banned delta-8 in 2026: it placed hemp-derived THC (other than low-dose delta-9) on the Texas Schedule I, even though those products were still federally legal hemp at the time.
