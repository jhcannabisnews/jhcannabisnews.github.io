The federal hemp ban, explained: what happens to delta-8, THCA and THC drinks, and when

In less than two months, most hemp THC gummies, drinks and flower could become unlawful under federal law in every state. The date you've probably heard is wrong. Here's exactly what changes, and when.

Setting it straight: our earlier episodes said delta-8 loses federal hemp status on November 12. That was the original plan. After Congress's September 2026 stopgap, most legal experts put delta-8 made from CBD on December 11 instead.

How it started

The 2018 Farm Bill legalized hemp, meaning cannabis with less than 0.3% delta-9 THC. It said nothing about other kinds of THC, so companies made delta-8, THCA flower and THC drinks instead, all technically hemp. The Associated Press now calls it a $28 billion industry.

In November 2025, the bill that ended the government shutdown included Section 781, which rewrites the definition of hemp:

  • Hemp is measured by total THC, which counts THCA.
  • Finished products are capped at 0.4 mg of total THC per container. Most THC drinks have 5 to 10 mg, so one drink is more than ten times the new limit.
  • Cannabinoids made outside the plant no longer count as hemp.

How it got delayed

The law was set to start November 12, 2026. This summer the White House pushed for a delay inside a must-pass spending bill. On August 8 the Senate voted 61–32 to kill Sen. Ted Budd's amendment to keep the November date, then passed the stopgap 90–6. The House passed it 370–48 on September 1, and it was signed September 2. According to Marijuana Moment, President Trump called Sen. Budd about it, and a White House official reportedly told Republican senators there would be no more extensions.

The two dates, product by product

November 12, 2026: cannabinoids the cannabis plant can't make on its own, fully lab-made ones like THC-O, lose federal hemp status.

December 11, 2026: everything else. THCA flower (total THC now counts THCA), delta-9 gummies and drinks over 0.4 mg per container, and delta-8 made from CBD, which most legal experts put here because it occurs naturally in tiny amounts. Some trackers still say November, so don't count on the extra month.

The surprise: some full-spectrum CBD carries trace THC and could cross 0.4 mg per container. President Trump has urged Congress to protect full-spectrum CBD. And the FDA lists of which cannabinoids count as natural, which Congress ordered, still haven't been published.

The fight in Washington

  • Ban it as written: Sen. Ted Budd (R-NC), who calls these products a "public health crisis," and a bipartisan coalition of state attorneys general.
  • Regulate it: a Klobuchar–Paul bill letting states with their own hemp rules opt out; a Rep. James Comer bill for another delay; and the Marijuana Policy Project, which wants 21-and-up sales and limits.

As of early October, none of those bills had a hearing or a vote. December 11 is also when government funding runs out, so any fix likely comes after the November 3 midterms.

What happens after December 11

How it plays out depends a lot on where you live. Some states already ban much of this, like Texas and Idaho. Others, like Minnesota and Kentucky, built their own rules for hemp THC. In Ohio, a federal judge ruled that only licensed marijuana dispensaries could sell THC drinks for the breweries in that lawsuit, a preview of one way this could go. One analyst told Forbes that billions in sales could shift to licensed dispensaries in states with legal marijuana. Check what's legal in your state →

What it means for you

  • THC drinks and gummies: December 11 is your date. Unless Congress acts, most won't meet the new limit.
  • THCA flower: also December 11. Total THC makes most of it marijuana under federal law.
  • Delta-8: plan for December 11 at the latest. In Texas it's already banned.
  • Shops and hemp growers: talk to a lawyer now, not in December.
