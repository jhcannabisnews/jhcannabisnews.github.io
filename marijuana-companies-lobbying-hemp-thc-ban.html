EPISODE 41 · MON NOV 23, 2026

Marijuana companies are lobbying for the hemp THC ban. Here's who

Some of the groups telling Congress to keep the Dec 11 hemp THC change on schedule represent licensed marijuana companies. Who lobbied, why, which legalization group broke with them, and what it means for Texas, where most adults have no dispensary.

Same plant, different rules. Whether it counts as hemp or marijuana is a legal line drawn by how much THC is in it. Licensed marijuana businesses pay heavy taxes and follow strict rules; hemp sellers under the 2018 Farm Bill have not. News, not legal advice.

Who lobbied

Cannabis Business Times reported, citing Politico, that in August the U.S. Cannabis Roundtable went to Washington to lobby against the hemp industry's push for regulation instead of prohibition. Its representatives came from licensed companies Trulieve, Curaleaf and Ascend Wellness and met with staff for four House members from both parties: Rep. Andy Harris, Rep. Virginia Foxx, Rep. Katherine Clark and Rep. Pete Aguilar. The American Trade Association for Cannabis and Hemp, which works for regulated cannabis businesses, lined up about 50 meetings with House and Senate offices in mid-September, joined by state marijuana trade groups from New Jersey, Missouri, Kentucky, Arizona and Ohio.

Their message: oppose further delays and let the closure take effect in December. They do not call it a ban. In their words, "Closing the loophole creates parity between cannabis and natural intoxicating hemp products" and "it's not a ban."

Why: taxes, rules and 280E

Cannabis Business Times laid out the money argument: licensed marijuana businesses carry heavy excise taxes, strict state rules, no selling across state lines and the federal tax rule 280E, which blocks normal deductions for sellers of Schedule I or II drugs. A hemp shop selling a THC drink under the 2018 Farm Bill does not. Backers of the closure also make a safety case: the trade association's Chris Lindsey told Stateline some hemp products can contain contaminants like pesticides, with no system to recall them. Testing and labels are fair concerns, which is why we back real rules. But hemp advocates say the motive is protecting licensed dispensaries' markets, while the marijuana groups say they are closing a loophole.

The legalization group that broke with them

The Marijuana Policy Project, which says it played a leading part in 14 of the 24 states with adult-use marijuana, asked supporters to send Congress a letter against the Dec 11 change, saying "regulation and education consistently yield better results than prohibition." Its sample letter asks for testing and regulation with a 21 age limit instead of what it says would make 95 percent of current hemp products illegal. So this is not marijuana versus hemp; it is one part of the cannabis world against another.

Illinois: the same line, a different route

Illinois uses the same federal line: more than 0.4 mg of total THC per container is not hemp but cannabis, sold only by a licensed dispensary. Hemp businesses sued, and last Thursday federal Judge Martha Pacold declined to pause the law, denying a temporary restraining order without prejudice. The next hearing is Dec 15. As reported, she noted the businesses would soon have to apply for a cannabis license anyway. Their lawyer said, "We would not be here today if we did not have a degree of confidence that Congress is going to act." Illinois has a legal marijuana market, so those products had a licensed counter to go to. In states without one, like Texas, there isn't.

Can Congress change the date?

The likeliest vehicle is the next funding bill, expected around Dec 11. Sens. Ted Budd (R-NC) and Pete Ricketts (R-NE) say they won't support one that overturns the ban. Sens. Tim Sheehy (R-MT) and Amy Klobuchar (D-MN) have a bipartisan plan to regulate hemp with a 21 age limit; it has not passed. Illinois's governor and Sen. Tammy Duckworth (D) want the federal ban on time, Marijuana Moment reported. As of Friday night nothing had changed the date.

What it means for Texas

For most adults, Texas has no dispensary: the only licensed marijuana sellers are the Compassionate Use Program's dispensing organizations, for patients with a prescription. Texas's 0.3 percent hemp rule is still on the books, but from Dec 11 federal law does not treat a product over the federal limit as hemp, and we found no ruling on whether shops can keep selling it. State health rules counting THCA remain in effect while the case is on appeal, and the 15th Court of Appeals canceled its December hearing. The Legislature meets January 12. Marijuana Policy Project executive director Adam Smith urged Texas lawmakers to regulate medical, adult-use and hemp together under one "safe and smart regulatory roof."

Dates to circle: Dec 11 (federal change), Dec 15 (Illinois hearing), Jan 12 (Texas Legislature). Contact lawmakers through our Contact Lawmakers page.

Frequently asked questions

Which marijuana groups lobbied for the hemp THC change?

Cannabis Business Times, citing Politico, reported the U.S. Cannabis Roundtable (with representatives from Trulieve, Curaleaf and Ascend Wellness) and the American Trade Association for Cannabis and Hemp, with state marijuana trade groups.

Why would licensed marijuana companies want hemp THC gone?

As reported, licensed businesses face heavy taxes, strict rules and 280E, which hemp sellers do not. The groups call it parity; hemp advocates say it protects dispensary markets.

Do all legalization groups agree?

No. The Marijuana Policy Project asked supporters to oppose the Dec 11 change and backs regulation with a 21 age limit instead.

What did the Illinois judge decide?

Judge Martha Pacold denied a temporary restraining order without prejudice, so the state law stands for now. The next hearing is Dec 15.
