EPISODE 39 · SAT NOV 21, 2026

Licensed medical marijuana is already Schedule III. So why is Texas different?

A DEA rule that took effect April 28, 2026 moved some marijuana to Schedule III. A federal schedule does not rewrite Texas criminal law, and one question is still open: does a Texas Compassionate Use Program license count?

The short version: the April rule puts marijuana in Schedule III only when it is in an FDA-approved drug product or subject to a state medical marijuana license. Everything else stays Schedule I. Texas criminal law is separate and did not change.

What the April rule actually says

The DEA rule was published in the Federal Register on April 28, 2026 and took effect that day. It moves marijuana, marijuana extract and natural delta-9 THC to Schedule III, but only in one of two situations: it is in a drug product the FDA has approved, or it is subject to a state medical marijuana license. Everything else stays in Schedule I, including marijuana sold under recreational-only licenses and marijuana in any state that has not licensed it for medical use. The rule ties this to the international drug treaty (licenses, prescriptions, import permits and quotas) and sets up a faster way for licensed businesses to register with the DEA.

It also addresses tax rule 280E, which blocks normal business deductions for sellers of Schedule I or II drugs. The rule says holders of state medical marijuana licenses will no longer be subject to it and asks the Treasury to consider relief for past years, while telling licensees to consult a tax professional.

Does a Texas license count?

The rule defines a state medical marijuana license as one issued by a state entity authorizing the licensee to manufacture, distribute or dispense marijuana or products containing it for medical purposes. Texas's Compassionate Use Program (TCUP) has DPS license a limited number of dispensing organizations to dispense low-THC cannabis to patients with qualifying conditions; DPS counts 180,299 patients listed since 2017, a lifetime count. On its face, that appears it could fit the definition. But the rule names no states, does not mention low-THC programs, and the DEA has not said which state programs count. We found nothing naming Texas.

Another open point: Texas caps TCUP products at 10 mg of THC per dose, but the federal hemp line is drawn by concentration, so whether a product counts as marijuana or hemp depends on its THC percentage. For TCUP patients, nothing in Texas law changed because of the April rule. If you are a licensee, that is a question for a lawyer and the DEA.

Why it is still a crime in Texas

Federal scheduling and state law are two separate systems. Schedule III is about federal rules: prescriptions, registrations, research and taxes. Under the Texas Health and Safety Code, possessing marijuana outside the medical program is still a crime; two ounces or less is a Class B misdemeanor, with penalties rising from there. The medical program is narrow: a qualifying condition, a registered doctor and low-THC cannabis from a licensed dispensing organization. HB 46 (2025) added chronic pain and set a 10 mg per-dose limit. The April rule did not change Texas criminal law and created no new state licenses. Why Texas is different is not the schedule; it is Texas's own law.

Can the rule survive?

Groups opposing rescheduling, including Smart Approaches to Marijuana, sued, and trade press reports the attorneys general of Nebraska and Indiana filed a challenge; the court combined the cases. They argue the rule skipped required process and expands access to marijuana. In September, Cannabis Business Times reported a federal appeals court refused to pause the rule while the case continues, which keeps it in effect but does not decide who wins. The Justice Department and supporters say it follows the federal health review and brings licensed medical businesses under federal oversight.

The bigger fight: all marijuana

The 2024 proposal would move all marijuana to Schedule III. It went through a hearing this summer before DEA Chief Administrative Law Judge Derek Julius, who in October admitted a government watchdog report and refused to reopen the hearing. Final briefs were due Nov 5. The judge next sends the record and a recommendation to DEA Administrator Terry Cole, who decides; any final rule can be challenged in court. Schedule III alone would not make marijuana legal to sell, and removing it from the schedules entirely would take Congress or a separate process. There is no date for any of it.

What to watch, and what would change Texas law

Watch the judge's recommendation and the administrator's decision; the Treasury on 280E relief; and whether any state's medical licensees, including Texas's, register through the DEA's new Medical Marijuana Dispensary Registration Portal. The rule says nothing about workplace drug testing, so check your employer's policy. The Dec 11 hemp change is a separate law from Congress. To change Texas law, the Legislature returns January 12: use our Contact Lawmakers page.

Frequently asked questions

Is marijuana Schedule III?

Only in two cases since April 28, 2026: when it is in an FDA-approved drug product or subject to a state medical marijuana license. Everything else stays Schedule I.

Is marijuana legal in Texas because of Schedule III?

No. A federal schedule does not change state criminal law. Possession outside the medical program is still a crime in Texas.

Does a Texas Compassionate Use Program license count under the DEA rule?

Unsettled. On its face the definition appears it could, but the rule names no states and the DEA has not said.

What is 280E?

A federal tax rule that blocks normal business deductions for sellers of Schedule I or II drugs. The April rule says state medical license holders are no longer subject to it.
